## The XYZ accomplishment formula

Write each experience bullet as "Accomplished X, as measured by Y, by doing Z."
Lead with the outcome (X), prove it with a number (Y), then name the action that
produced it (Z). "Cut checkout latency 40% (Y) by rewriting the payments queue (Z)"
beats "Responsible for payments performance." A bullet that names a result the
reader can verify is more persuasive than one that only lists duties.

## Start every bullet with a strong action verb

Open with a past-tense action verb that shows ownership: built, led, shipped,
reduced, negotiated, automated, launched. Avoid weak openers — "responsible for",
"worked on", "helped with", "involved in" — which describe presence, not impact.
One verb per bullet; do not stack two ("helped build and manage").

## Quantify impact, and never invent the number

A number turns a claim into evidence: percent change, money, time saved, scale
(users, requests, team size), or rank. Prefer the metric that matters to the
target role. If the candidate did not supply a number, do NOT fabricate one —
keep the bullet qualitative and concrete instead. A real "reduced manual review
time" is honest; a made-up "reduced it 35%" is a fireable misrepresentation.

## Tailor the bullet to the target job description

Mirror the language of the JD where it is truthful. If the role asks for
"distributed systems" and the candidate built one, use that phrasing rather than a
private internal codename. Tailoring is selection and rewording of true facts to
match the reader — never adding experience the candidate does not have.

## Make bullets ATS-safe

Applicant Tracking Systems parse plain text. Avoid tables, text boxes, images,
columns, and graphics for content that must be read — they scramble or vanish on
parse. Use a single-column layout, standard section headings (Experience,
Education, Skills), and a common font. Put skills as plain words, not in a chart.

## One page for early career, two at most for senior

Recruiters skim. Cut anything older than ~10 years or irrelevant to the target
role. Early-career and most individual-contributor CVs belong on one page; deep
senior or academic histories may justify two. Density should never push the font
below readable size — trim content instead of shrinking type.

## Cut filler and hedging words

Remove "various", "several", "successfully", "in order to", and adjectives that
add no information ("dynamic professional"). Every word should carry a fact.
"Successfully delivered" says no more than "delivered" — drop the adverb. Tighter
bullets read as more senior.

## Lead with the most impressive bullet in each role

Order bullets within a role by impact, not chronology. The first line under each
job is the one most readers actually finish, so it should be the strongest result.
Supporting or routine bullets go lower, or get cut if the role is already well
evidenced.

## Match seniority signals to the target level

A bullet's verbs and scope should match the level being targeted. For senior roles,
surface scope and influence (led a team of N, owned a P&L, set the roadmap); for
early roles, surface execution and learning velocity. Do not inflate — claiming
leadership the candidate did not hold is the fastest way to fail an interview.

## Keep tense and person consistent

Past roles use past tense; the current role may use present tense for ongoing work.
Never write in the first person ("I built") or use pronouns — CV bullets are
implied-subject fragments. Consistent tense and voice read as careful and
professional; mixed tense reads as careless.

## Separate the final rewrite from mentor thinking

When rewriting a CV bullet, the accepted artifact must be one polished final
rewrite of the bullet. Any mentor thinking, rationale, checks, or caveats belong
in a secondary disclosure for the user to inspect, not in the rewrite itself.
The final rewrite should be a concise CV bullet that can replace the original
line directly. Do not put prompt analysis, self-questioning, source commentary,
or drafting notes in the final rewrite. If the source facts are thin, keep the
final rewrite truthful and qualitative rather than inventing details; the accept
action applies only to the final rewrite.
